Estimating Shots and Variance

arXiv:2501.03194 • 6 January 2025

A quantum computer hands you one sample from a probability distribution shaped by your circuit, not the answer directly. Run the circuit once and you get one string of bits; run it again and you get a different one. Each run is called a shot, and the number you actually wanted, usually an average, has to be assembled out of thousands of shots. Time on these machines is rented by the second, so every shot has a price. Too few shots and the answer is noise; too many and you paid for precision you did not need.

Given a circuit, a noisy machine, and a target precision, can the number of shots be predicted in advance, instead of found by trial and error, which is how it is mostly done today? It can be done in two ways:

  • Bottom-Up: take one qubit, model each noise source from physics, and predict the spread of its outcomes from the machine's published calibration data.
  • Top-Down: for real multi-qubit circuits, where the bottom-up route turns out to be intractable, fit a two-parameter law to a few cheap runs and read the shot count off the curve.

A coin made of one qubit

The simplest useful circuit is one qubit, one Hadamard gate, one measurement. The qubit starts in ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩, the HHH gate puts it in an equal superposition, and the measurement collapses it to 0 or 1 with probability one half each. On a perfect machine this is a fair coin, and most of the bottom-up approach is us studying how noise bends this coin.

Averaging the bits over NNN shots gives an estimate μ\muμ of the true probability μ0=0.5\mu_0 = 0.5μ0​=0.5. One shot is a Bernoulli draw, so its variance is p(1−p)p(1-p)p(1−p), and averaging NNN independent draws divides the variance by NNN:

one shot:σ2=p(1−p)=0.5×0.5=0.25N shots:σ=0.25/N=0.5/NN=100:σ=0.05N=10 000:σ=0.005 \begin{aligned} \text{one shot:}\quad \sigma^2 &= p(1-p) = 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25\\ N \text{ shots:}\quad \sigma &= \sqrt{0.25/N} = 0.5/\sqrt{N}\\ N = 100:\quad \sigma &= 0.05\\ N = 10\,000:\quad \sigma &= 0.005 \end{aligned} one shot:σ2N shots:σN=100:σN=10000:σ​=p(1−p)=0.5×0.5=0.25=0.25/N​=0.5/N​=0.05=0.005​

The spread shrinks as 1/N1/\sqrt{N}1/N​, so each extra digit of precision costs a factor of 100 in shots, which is to say a factor of 100 in money. This square-root law is the reason shot estimation is worth doing carefully: overshooting "to be safe" is expensive in the same regime where the machine is already expensive.

Noise that hides and noise that shifts

The first noise source is the most mundane: the machine sometimes reads the bit wrong. These are state preparation and measurement (SPAM) errors, and the readout half of them comes in two parts, p0→1p_{0 \to 1}p0→1​, the probability a 0 is reported as 1, and p1→0p_{1 \to 0}p1→0​, the reverse. The measured mean becomes

μ′=P(1)+p0→1 P(0)−p1→0 P(1) \mu' = P(1) + p_{0\to1}\,P(0) - p_{1\to0}\,P(1) μ′=P(1)+p0→1​P(0)−p1→0​P(1)

For the fair coin P(0)=P(1)=0.5P(0) = P(1) = 0.5P(0)=P(1)=0.5. Take a strongly asymmetric case, p0→1=0.33p_{0\to1} = 0.33p0→1​=0.33 and p1→0=0.5p_{1\to0} = 0.5p1→0​=0.5:

μ′=0.5+0.33×0.5−0.5×0.5=0.415 \mu' = 0.5 + 0.33 \times 0.5 - 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.415 μ′=0.5+0.33×0.5−0.5×0.5=0.415

Two things follow, and you can check both in the widget below. If the two error rates are equal, the shifts cancel and μ′=0.5\mu' = 0.5μ′=0.5 exactly: the mean of a fair coin cannot see symmetric readout noise at all, no matter how large it is. If the rates differ, the whole distribution slides to a new centre, 0.415 in the worked case, and no number of shots brings it back. More shots only sharpen the estimate of the wrong value. The symmetric cancellation holds on two independent noisy simulators, IBM's and CERN's, both set to p0→1=p1→0=0.33p_{0\to1} = p_{1\to0} = 0.33p0→1​=p1→0​=0.33.

One number per qubit

To compare spreads across different means we switch from σ\sigmaσ to the relative standard deviation, RSD=σ/μ\text{RSD} = \sigma/\muRSD=σ/μ, which is dimensionless. Then we apply the central limit theorem in its most practical form. Take a long record of shots, 2152^{15}215 of them, and chop it into 256 windows of www shots each, for w=4,8,16,…,128w = 4, 8, 16, \ldots, 128w=4,8,16,…,128. Average each window. The window averages are close to normally distributed, and their RSD inherits the square-root law, so on log axes the points fall on a straight line:

ln⁡(RSD)=−12ln⁡w+c \ln(\text{RSD}) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\ln w + c ln(RSD)=−21​lnw+c

The slope is always −1/2-1/2−1/2; it carries no information about the device. Everything the noise does to the distribution is compressed into the intercept ccc. For a coin that reads 1 with probability ppp, the window average has mean ppp and standard deviation p(1−p)/w\sqrt{p(1-p)/w}p(1−p)/w​, so the intercept can be computed in closed form (all logs natural here):

c=ln⁡σμ+12ln⁡w=ln⁡p(1−p)/wp+12ln⁡w=12ln⁡1−pp=12ln⁡p0p1 \begin{aligned} c &= \ln\frac{\sigma}{\mu} + \tfrac{1}{2}\ln w = \ln\frac{\sqrt{p(1-p)/w}}{p} + \tfrac{1}{2}\ln w\\ &= \tfrac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1-p}{p} = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln\frac{p_0}{p_1} \end{aligned} c​=lnμσ​+21​lnw=lnpp(1−p)/w​​+21​lnw=21​lnp1−p​=21​lnp1​p0​​​

A fair coin has p0=p1p_0 = p_1p0​=p1​ and therefore c=0c = 0c=0. The asymmetric SPAM coin from the last section had μ′=0.415\mu' = 0.415μ′=0.415, so

c=12ln⁡0.5850.415=0.172 c = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln\frac{0.585}{0.415} = 0.172 c=21​ln0.4150.585​=0.172

and substituting μ′\mu'μ′ for ppp in general gives the prediction straight from the two readout rates:

cpred=12ln⁡ ⁣(1+p1→0−p0→11+p0→1−p1→0) c_{\text{pred}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln\!\left(\frac{1 + p_{1\to0} - p_{0\to1}}{1 + p_{0\to1} - p_{1\to0}}\right) cpred​=21​ln(1+p0→1​−p1→0​1+p1→0​−p0→1​​)

Symmetric errors give cpred=0c_{\text{pred}} = 0cpred​=0 regardless of size, matching what the mean already told us. The formula is also a warning: if one error rate is held at zero and the other is xxx, the sensitivity is dc/dx=±2/(x2−1)dc/dx = \pm 2/(x^2 - 1)dc/dx=±2/(x2−1), which is steepest near small xxx. One-directional readout error inflates the variance fastest, so a hardware designer should want readout errors small and symmetric, in that order of importance.

This is the entire bottom-up program in miniature: predict ccc from the calibration sheet, measure ccc from the windowed shots, compare. Every quantum machine publishes a calibration sheet per qubit on every calibration cycle: readout error rates, decay times, gate errors. On IBM's Torino machine the prediction landed within 0.01 of the measured intercept using nothing but that sheet.

The decay clocks

A qubit holding ∣1⟩|1\rangle∣1⟩ relaxes toward ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩ with a characteristic time T1T_1T1​. To see it with the coin, we insert a wait of length ttt between the HHH gate and the measurement. The probability that an excited qubit decays during the wait is ε=1−e−t/T1\varepsilon = 1 - e^{-t/T_1}ε=1−e−t/T1​, which reshuffles the two outcome probabilities:

p0′=p0+p1 ε,p1′=p1 (1−ε) p_0' = p_0 + p_1\,\varepsilon, \qquad p_1' = p_1\,(1 - \varepsilon) p0′​=p0​+p1​ε,p1′​=p1​(1−ε)

Pushing these through the intercept formula gives the same expression as SPAM with one new factor bbb:

cpred=12ln⁡ ⁣(b (1−p0→1)+p1→0b p0→1+1−p1→0),b=1+ε1−ε c_{\text{pred}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln\!\left(\frac{b\,(1-p_{0\to1}) + p_{1\to0}}{b\,p_{0\to1} + 1 - p_{1\to0}}\right), \qquad b = \frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon} cpred​=21​ln(bp0→1​+1−p1→0​b(1−p0→1​)+p1→0​​),b=1−ε1+ε​

When the wait is short, ε→0\varepsilon \to 0ε→0, b→1b \to 1b→1, and the SPAM formula reappears unchanged. With clean readout the formula collapses to c=12ln⁡bc = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln bc=21​lnb, and the numbers stay tame for realistic waits. Torino's calibration listed T1=232T_1 = 232T1​=232 microseconds; a wait one tenth of that gives

ε=1−e−23.2/232=0.095b=1.0950.905=1.210c=12ln⁡1.210=0.095 \begin{aligned} \varepsilon &= 1 - e^{-23.2/232} = 0.095\\ b &= \frac{1.095}{0.905} = 1.210\\ c &= \tfrac{1}{2}\ln 1.210 = 0.095 \end{aligned} εbc​=1−e−23.2/232=0.095=0.9051.095​=1.210=21​ln1.210=0.095​

Running the wait circuit on Torino's hardware across a range of delays and comparing to these predictions, the median error was 0.01.

The second clock, T2T_2T2​, governs phase: how long the qubit remembers where in the superposition it is, not just how much. Phase is invisible to a direct measurement, so the circuit changes to HHH, wait, HHH. With no wait the two Hadamards cancel and the circuit always reads 0. As the wait grows the surviving coherence ε2=e−t/T2\varepsilon_2 = e^{-t/T_2}ε2​=e−t/T2​ shrinks, and the outcome drifts from deterministic toward a fair coin. The same intercept formula applies with b=(1+ε2)/(1−ε2)b = (1+\varepsilon_2)/(1-\varepsilon_2)b=(1+ε2​)/(1−ε2​), and on Torino the predictions held with mean and median errors of about 1%, with a visibly larger spread than the T1T_1T1​ case.

Where the decay factor b comes from

The clean way to track both decays at once is the Jaynes-Cummings density matrix. Write the qubit state as α∣0⟩+β∣1⟩\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangleα∣0⟩+β∣1⟩ and apply the standard T1,T2T_1, T_2T1​,T2​ noise channel for a wait of length ttt; with ε1=e−t/T1\varepsilon_1 = e^{-t/T_1}ε1​=e−t/T1​ and ε2=e−t/T2\varepsilon_2 = e^{-t/T_2}ε2​=e−t/T2​ the state becomes

ρT1+T2=(1+(∣α∣2−1) ε1αβ∗ ε2α∗β ε2∣β∣2 ε1) \rho_{T_1+T_2} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + (|\alpha|^2 - 1)\,\varepsilon_1 & \alpha\beta^*\,\varepsilon_2\\ \alpha^*\beta\,\varepsilon_2 & |\beta|^2\,\varepsilon_1 \end{pmatrix} ρT1​+T2​​=(1+(∣α∣2−1)ε1​α∗βε2​​αβ∗ε2​∣β∣2ε1​​)

The diagonal carries populations and is eaten by ε1\varepsilon_1ε1​; the off-diagonal carries coherence and is eaten by ε2\varepsilon_2ε2​. A direct measurement reads only the diagonal, which is why the T1T_1T1​ experiment needs no extra gates. Sandwiching the wait between Hadamards rotates the off-diagonal terms onto the diagonal by creating a change of basis, so the second measurement reads the surviving coherence as a bias of the coin.

One more refinement, because the calibration sheet is itself a measurement: T2T_2T2​ comes with an uncertainty σT2\sigma_{T_2}σT2​​, and that uncertainty propagates into the predicted intercept through the derivative,

σc=∂c∂T2 σT2=t et/T2T22(e2t/T2−1) σT2At T2=500 δt,    t=100δt,    σT2=20 δt:σc=100⋅e0.25002(e0.4−1)⋅20≈122.1122 950⋅20≈0.02 \begin{aligned} \sigma_c &= \frac{\partial c}{\partial T_2}\,\sigma_{T_2} = \frac{t\,e^{t/T_2}}{T_2^2\left(e^{2t/T_2} - 1\right)}\,\sigma_{T_2} \\ \text{At } &T_2 = 500\,\delta t,\;\; t = 100\delta t,\;\; \sigma_{T_2} = 20\, \delta t: \\ \sigma_c &= \frac{100 \cdot e^{0.2}}{500^2 \left(e^{0.4} - 1\right)} \cdot 20 \approx \frac{122.1}{122\,950} \cdot 20 \approx 0.02 \end{aligned} σc​At σc​​=∂T2​∂c​σT2​​=T22​(e2t/T2​−1)tet/T2​​σT2​​T2​=500δt,t=100δt,σT2​​=20δt:=5002(e0.4−1)100⋅e0.2​⋅20≈122950122.1​⋅20≈0.02​

where δt\delta tδt is the machine's pulse time unit. The factor 1/T221/T_2^21/T22​ means the uncertainty in ccc dies quickly as coherence times improve, and t→0t \to 0t→0 gives σc→0\sigma_c \to 0σc​→0: short circuits are insensitive to how well you know the clock.

Gates lie too

Gate errors are the third ingredient. Assuming no correlated errors, each imperfect gate acts like a depolarising channel: with some probability it applies an unwanted XXX, YYY or ZZZ to the qubit. Individual probabilities per axis are hard to extract, but randomised benchmarking publishes a single combined figure, the error per layered gate EEE, and splitting it evenly gives px=py=pz=E/3p_x = p_y = p_z = E/3px​=py​=pz​=E/3. Errors along a given axis compound across kkk gates as

Pk=1−(1−p)k≈k p P_k = 1 - (1-p)^k \approx k\,p Pk​=1−(1−p)k≈kp

which for p=0.001p = 0.001p=0.001 and k=50k = 50k=50 gives 1−0.99950=0.04881 - 0.999^{50} = 0.04881−0.99950=0.0488 against the shortcut's 0.050.050.05. Substituting these compounded probabilities into the same intercept machinery, and running on Torino's hardware, the prediction errors stayed within about 0.1, with a slight bias toward underprediction. These figures do not account for SPAM, so part of that 0.1 is noise deliberately left out of the model.

The shortcut is safe as long as k≤1/pk \le 1/pk≤1/p. Past that the binomial error term grows large enough to corrupt predictions even though the circuit itself may still be fine. So the circuit still might work, but my math stops working.

Where the bottom-up story ends

At this point one qubit is a solved bookkeeping exercise: every noise source on the calibration sheet maps to a predictable shift of one number, ccc. This however can be used only to caliberate individual qubits, and not compose them into large circuits.

The variance of a sum of two random bits is

Var(X1+X2)=Var(X1)+Var(X2)+2 Cov(X1,X2) \mathrm{Var}(X_1 + X_2) = \mathrm{Var}(X_1) + \mathrm{Var}(X_2) + 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X_1, X_2) Var(X1​+X2​)=Var(X1​)+Var(X2​)+2Cov(X1​,X2​)

For classical independent coins the covariance is zero and life is easy. Qubits in one circuit are entangled; entanglement is correlation, so the covariance term cannot be dropped. Every pair of qubits needs its own covariance, and pairs grow quadratically:

(1002)=100×992=4950 \binom{100}{2} = \frac{100 \times 99}{2} = 4950 (2100​)=2100×99​=4950

covariances for a 100-qubit circuit, each one requiring its own measurements, on top of the single-qubit terms. Worse, the answer depends on how the measured bits are combined into a number, before any noise is even considered.

How bits combine decides the variance

Take kkk independent bits where a 1 flips to 0 with probability ppp and a 0 flips to 1 with probability qqq. The variance contributed by the bit in position iii is

Var(Xi)=(1−p+q)(p−q)⋅2i \mathrm{Var}(X_i) = (1 - p + q)(p - q)\cdot 2^i Var(Xi​)=(1−p+q)(p−q)⋅2i

If the bits are read as an integer, 1010102=42101010_2 = 421010102​=42, the 2i2^i2i weights make the variance scale exponentially with position, irrespective of ppp and qqq: one flipped high bit moves the answer by half its range. If the same bits are read as a binary fraction, 0.01101012≈0.420.0110101_2 \approx 0.420.01101012​≈0.42, as the phase estimation algorithm does, each extra bit only refines the answer and the variance stays small. For a product of two variables the mixing is worse still:

Var(X1X2)=(Var(X1)+μ12)(Var(X2)+μ22)−μ12 μ22 \mathrm{Var}(X_1 X_2) = \left(\mathrm{Var}(X_1) + \mu_1^2\right)\left(\mathrm{Var}(X_2) + \mu_2^2\right) - \mu_1^2\,\mu_2^2 Var(X1​X2​)=(Var(X1​)+μ12​)(Var(X2​)+μ22​)−μ12​μ22​

means and variances contaminate each other. So even with a full table of single-qubit characterisations, the circuit-level variance depends on the readout convention, the combination arithmetic, and every pairwise covariance. There is no universal formula to aim for.

Measure the variance instead

Here we flip direction; instead of predicting the variance from parts, measure it whole, but measure it cheaply. The analysis restricts to the class of circuits most NISQ algorithms already live in: expectation-value circuits, where the goal is the average ⟨O⟩\langle O \rangle⟨O⟩ of some observable OOO over a prepared state. Variational chemistry, optimisation, most near-term workloads have this shape. For such circuits the observable's intrinsic variance is itself measurable:

σ2=Var(O)=⟨O2⟩−⟨O⟩2 \sigma^2 = \mathrm{Var}(O) = \langle O^2 \rangle - \langle O \rangle^2 σ2=Var(O)=⟨O2⟩−⟨O⟩2

and on a noiseless machine the estimator after NNN shots obeys the textbook law Var(EˉN)=σ2/N\mathrm{Var}(\bar E_N) = \sigma^2/NVar(EˉN​)=σ2/N: a straight line of slope −1-1−1 on log-log axes, falling forever. On a real machine it does not fall forever, and the deviation is modelled by the measured variance as

Var(EˉN)=AN+B,A≈σ2,B≥0 \mathrm{Var}(\bar E_N) = \frac{A}{N} + B, \qquad A \approx \sigma^2,\quad B \ge 0 Var(EˉN​)=NA​+B,A≈σ2,B≥0

AAA is the statistical part, the intrinsic quantum randomness that shots genuinely average away. BBB is a bias floor from systematic, device- and circuit-dependent error, the multi-qubit descendant of the intercept ccc: shots do nothing to it. The model is two numbers, and both come almost free. The same procedure that estimates ⟨O⟩\langle O \rangle⟨O⟩ can also be used to estimate ⟨O2⟩\langle O^2 \rangle⟨O2⟩ with the same shot data. With one additional run of the same circuit with a 'squared' observable, the variance can be estimated. The fit over a few shot counts then gives us AAA and BBB.

A molecule as the test bench

The test bench is the standard entry-level chemistry problem: a variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) finding the ground-state energy of the H2H_2H2​ molecule, four qubits, a Hartree-Fock starting state, and an excitation-preserving ansatz. The observable is the molecular Hamiltonian, estimated term by term from ±1\pm 1±1-eigenvalue measurements.

The actual Hamiltonian

The variance does carry its own price: ⟨O2⟩\langle O^2 \rangle⟨O2⟩ means measuring a squared Hamiltonian, which compiles to its own, longer list of Pauli strings, each needing shots. The saving is that this overhead does not have to match the size of the main run. The variance enters only through the fitted AAA and BBB, and the curve, once fitted at small shot counts, can be evaluated at any NNN. So if the production run spends 50,000 shots on one precise expectation value, learning its variance does not take a second 50,000-shot campaign; the cheap fit already prices it:

Var(Eˉ50 000)=5650 000+0.002≈0.0031 \mathrm{Var}(\bar E_{50\,000}) = \frac{56}{50\,000} + 0.002 \approx 0.0031 Var(Eˉ50000​)=5000056​+0.002≈0.0031

The expensive run buys the precision; a handful of cheap runs is enough to certify it.

Both fitted numbers are perishable. AAA varies with the state being prepared, BBB with the device, the qubits chosen, the layout, and the hour; the two-day-stale calibration that wrecked the qubit 61 prediction applies with full force here. Measurements need to be made afresh, per session. Two gaps however still remain open. Correlated errors and crosstalk are excluded for lack of accepted models, and there is no mechanistic account of BBB: the bottom-up half of the paper explains its single-qubit ancestor ccc from first principles, but what sets the size of the multi-qubit floor, and which knobs lower it, is still an empirical question.
